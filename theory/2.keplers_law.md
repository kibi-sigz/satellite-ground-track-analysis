# 02 — Kepler's Laws of Planetary Motion

## 1. Introduction

Kepler's laws describe the motion of celestial bodies under gravitational attraction. They provide the mathematical foundation for understanding how artificial satellites move around Earth.

There are three fundamental laws:

1. The Law of Orbits.
2. The Law of Areas.
3. The Law of Periods.

These laws are essential for predicting spacecraft position, orbital velocity and orbital period.

## 2. Kepler's First Law — The Law of Orbits

Kepler's First Law states:

**Every planet or satellite moves in an elliptical orbit, with the central attracting body located at one of the two foci of the ellipse.**

For an elliptical orbit, the radial distance from Earth to the spacecraft is:

$$
r = \frac{p}{1+e\cos\theta}
$$

Where:

- \(r\) — distance between Earth and spacecraft.
- \(p\) — semi-latus rectum.
- \(e\) — orbital eccentricity.
- \(\theta\) — true anomaly.

The semi-latus rectum is calculated as:

$$
p=a(1-e^2)
$$

For Variant 7:

$$
p=7121(1-0.007021^2)
$$

Therefore:

$$
\boxed{p\approx7120.65\text{ km}}
$$

The orbital radius changes continuously as the spacecraft moves along its elliptical trajectory.

## 3. Kepler's Second Law — The Law of Areas

Kepler's Second Law states:

**A line connecting a satellite to the centre of Earth sweeps out equal areas during equal intervals of time.**

This means that the spacecraft does not move at a constant speed throughout an elliptical orbit.

- At perigee, the spacecraft moves fastest.
- At apogee, the spacecraft moves slowest.

The mathematical expression is:

$$
\frac{dA}{dt}=\frac{h}{2}
$$

Where:

- \(A\) — swept area.
- \(t\) — time.
- \(h\) — specific angular momentum.

Specific angular momentum is:

$$
h=\sqrt{\mu p}
$$

Where \(\mu\) is Earth's gravitational parameter.

For Earth:

$$
\mu=398600.44\text{ km}^3/\text{s}^2
$$

This law explains why spacecraft velocity changes along an elliptical orbit.

## 4. Kepler's Third Law — The Law of Periods

Kepler's Third Law establishes a relationship between orbital period and semi-major axis.

The orbital period is calculated using:

$$
T=2\pi\sqrt{\frac{a^3}{\mu}}
$$

Where:

- \(T\) — orbital period in seconds.
- \(a\) — semi-major axis in kilometres.
- \(\mu\) — gravitational parameter of Earth.

For Variant 7:

$$
T=2\pi\sqrt{\frac{7121^3}{398600.44}}
$$

Therefore:

$$
\boxed{T\approx5980.3\text{ seconds}}
$$

Converting to minutes:

$$
T=\frac{5980.3}{60}
$$

$$
\boxed{T\approx99.67\text{ minutes}}
$$

This means each spacecraft completes approximately one revolution around Earth every 99.67 minutes under the ideal two-body model.

## 5. Mean Motion

Mean motion represents the average angular velocity of a spacecraft over one complete orbital revolution.

It is calculated as:

$$
n=\frac{2\pi}{T}
$$

For Variant 7:

$$
n=\frac{2\pi}{5980.3}
$$

Therefore:

$$
\boxed{n\approx0.0010506\text{ rad/s}}
$$

Mean motion is used to calculate the mean anomaly at any given time.

## 6. Mean Anomaly

Mean anomaly describes the angular progression of a spacecraft as if it were moving uniformly around a circular reference orbit with the same orbital period.

It is calculated as:

$$
M=M_0+nt
$$

Where:

- \(M\) — mean anomaly at time \(t\).
- \(M_0\) — initial mean anomaly.
- \(n\) — mean motion.
- \(t\) — elapsed time.

Mean anomaly is useful because it increases uniformly with time.

However, it is not the actual angular position of the spacecraft in an elliptical orbit.

## 7. Eccentric Anomaly

Eccentric anomaly is an auxiliary angular parameter used to describe the position of a spacecraft on an elliptical orbit.

It is represented by:

$$
E
$$

The relationship between mean anomaly and eccentric anomaly is given by Kepler's equation:

$$
M=E-e\sin E
$$

This equation is nonlinear and generally cannot be solved directly using elementary algebra.

Therefore, numerical methods are used.

## 8. Solving Kepler's Equation Using Newton-Raphson

The Newton-Raphson method is an iterative numerical technique used to find approximate solutions to nonlinear equations.

Starting with:

$$
f(E)=E-e\sin E-M
$$

Its derivative is:

$$
f'(E)=1-e\cos E
$$

The Newton-Raphson iteration becomes:

$$
E_{k+1}=E_k-
\frac{E_k-e\sin E_k-M}
{1-e\cos E_k}
$$

Where:

- \(E_k\) — current estimate.
- \(E_{k+1}\) — improved estimate.
- \(e\) — eccentricity.
- \(M\) — mean anomaly.

The process continues until the difference between successive estimates becomes sufficiently small.

This is the numerical approach used in our Python orbital simulation.

## 9. True Anomaly

True anomaly describes the actual angular position of the spacecraft relative to the perigee direction.

It is represented by:

$$
\theta
$$

The relationship between eccentric anomaly and true anomaly is:

$$
\theta =
2\operatorname{atan2}
\left(
\sqrt{1+e}\sin\frac{E}{2},
\sqrt{1-e}\cos\frac{E}{2}
\right)
$$

The `atan2` function helps determine the correct angular quadrant.

The true anomaly is used to calculate the instantaneous orbital radius and spacecraft position.

## 11. Conclusion

Kepler's laws provide the mathematical foundation for predicting spacecraft motion.

The orbital period and mean motion describe the time characteristics of the orbit, while mean anomaly, eccentric anomaly and true anomaly describe the spacecraft's angular position.

By solving Kepler's equation numerically, a computer program can calculate the spacecraft's position at successive points in time.

These calculations form the foundation of the coordinate transformations and ground-track generation discussed in the next chapter.
