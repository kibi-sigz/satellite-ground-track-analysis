# 04 — Satellite Ground-Track Analysis

## 1. Introduction

A satellite ground track is the trajectory traced by the subsatellite point across Earth's surface as the spacecraft moves along its orbit.

The subsatellite point is the geographic location directly beneath the satellite.

Ground-track analysis is important in satellite mission planning because it helps determine:

- Which geographical regions a satellite passes over.
- How frequently a region can be observed.
- The geographical distribution of satellite coverage.
- Opportunities for communication with ground stations.
- The observation periods available for selected locations.

In this project, ground tracks are calculated for five spacecraft with different Right Ascension of the Ascending Node (RAAN) values.

## 2. What Determines a Ground Track?

A satellite ground track depends on several orbital and Earth-related parameters:

1. Orbital inclination.
2. Orbital altitude and eccentricity.
3. RAAN.
4. Argument of perigee.
5. Initial true anomaly.
6. Earth's rotational motion.
7. The initial reference epoch.

The spacecraft moves along its orbital trajectory while Earth rotates beneath it.

Consequently, the ground track is different from the orbit itself.

## 3. Subsatellite Point

The subsatellite point is obtained by projecting the spacecraft's position onto Earth's surface along the radial direction from Earth's centre.

For the spherical Earth approximation, the satellite's geographic coordinates are calculated using its ECEF position.

Latitude:

$$
\phi=
\operatorname{atan2}
\left(
z,\sqrt{x^2+y^2}
\right)
$$

Longitude:

$$
\lambda=\operatorname{atan2}(y,x)
$$

Where \(x\), \(y\), and \(z\) are the satellite's ECEF coordinates.

The resulting coordinates describe the subsatellite point.

## 4. Effect of Earth's Rotation

Earth rotates around its polar axis while the spacecraft moves along its orbit.

Earth's angular velocity is approximately:

$$
\omega_E=7.2921159\times10^{-5}
\text{ rad/s}
$$

The angular rotation of Earth after elapsed time \(t\) is:

$$
\theta_E=\omega_E t
$$

For one complete orbital revolution:

$$
\Delta\theta_E=\omega_E T
$$

Using the orbital period calculated in Chapter 2:

$$
T\approx5980.3\text{ s}
$$

Therefore:

$$
\Delta\theta_E=
7.2921159\times10^{-5}\times5980.3
$$

$$
\boxed{\Delta\theta_E\approx24.99^\circ}
$$

This means Earth rotates approximately 24.99 degrees during one satellite revolution.

For a prograde orbit, the ground track consequently shifts westward relative to Earth's surface between successive revolutions, under this simplified model.

## 5. Ground Tracks of Multiple Spacecraft

This project considers five spacecraft with identical orbital size and inclination but different RAAN values.

The orbital parameters are:

| Spacecraft | Inclination | RAAN |
|---|---:|---:|
| SC-1 | 80° | 0° |
| SC-2 | 80° | 72° |
| SC-3 | 80° | 144° |
| SC-4 | 80° | 216° |
| SC-5 | 80° | 288° |

The RAAN values are separated by:

$$
\Delta\Omega=\frac{360^\circ}{5}
$$

$$
\boxed{\Delta\Omega=72^\circ}
$$

### 5.1 Why Do the Spacecraft Have Different Ground Tracks?

RAAN determines the orientation of the orbital plane around Earth's equatorial plane.

Although all five spacecraft have the same inclination, their different RAAN values mean that their orbital planes have different orientations.

Consequently, the spacecraft follow different spatial trajectories around Earth.

Their corresponding subsatellite points therefore produce different ground-track patterns.

### 5.2 Important Distinction

Different RAAN values produce different orbital planes.

Different initial true anomalies, on the other hand, change the initial positions of spacecraft within their respective orbital planes.

These are two different ways of distributing spacecraft in an orbital configuration.

## 6. Ground-Track Longitude Convention

Longitude is commonly expressed in either of two conventions:

- \(-180^\circ\) to \(+180^\circ\).
- \(0^\circ\) to \(360^\circ\).

This project uses the second convention.

The conversion is:

$$
\lambda_{360}=
(\lambda+360^\circ)\bmod360^\circ
$$

This ensures that negative longitude values are represented as positive angles between 0° and 360°.

When plotting ground tracks, special attention must be given to longitude discontinuities.

For example, a trajectory crossing the longitude boundary may appear to jump from approximately 359° to 0°.

This is a coordinate wraparound, not a physical jump in the spacecraft trajectory. The plotting algorithm should break the line at such discontinuities.

## 7. Numerical Sampling of the Ground Track

A computer cannot draw a continuous trajectory directly from a mathematical equation.

Instead, it calculates spacecraft positions at discrete time intervals.

For this project, the initial simulation uses:

$$
\Delta t=30\text{ seconds}
$$

The time sequence is:

$$
t_k=k\Delta t
$$

Where:

- \(t_k\) is the sampling time.
- \(k\) is the sample index.
- \(\Delta t\) is the sampling interval.

At every time step, the program calculates the spacecraft's orbital position and converts it into geographic coordinates.

The resulting latitude and longitude values are stored and used to construct the ground-track plot.

A smaller time interval provides more closely spaced trajectory points but increases the number of calculations and output records.

## 8. Ground-Track Repetition

A ground track does not necessarily repeat after one orbital revolution.

This is because Earth rotates while the spacecraft completes its orbit.

For an idealised orbit with constant orbital period and uniform Earth rotation, a ground-track repeat occurs when the satellite completes an integer number of revolutions while Earth completes an integer number of rotations relative to the inertial frame.

The condition can be expressed as:

$$
N T = D T_E
$$

Where:

- \(N\) is the number of satellite revolutions.
- \(D\) is the number of Earth rotations.
- \(T\) is the satellite orbital period.
- \(T_E\) is Earth's rotational period.

For realistic missions, orbital perturbations and variations in Earth's orientation also affect ground-track repetition.

## 9. Application to Variant 7

The purpose of the Variant 7 simulation is to calculate and visualise the ground tracks of five spacecraft.

The computational procedure is:

1. Define the orbital parameters for all five spacecraft.
2. Calculate the orbital period and mean motion.
3. Generate a sequence of observation times.
4. Solve Kepler's equation at each time step.
5. Calculate the true anomaly and orbital radius.
6. Transform spacecraft coordinates into ECI.
7. Account for Earth's rotation and calculate ECEF coordinates.
8. Convert ECEF coordinates into latitude and longitude.
9. Store the calculated positions.
10. Plot the ground tracks of all five spacecraft on the same geographic representation.

The resulting visualisation allows the spatial distribution of the spacecraft trajectories to be compared.

## 10. Modelling Limitations

The initial implementation uses a simplified mathematical model.

The following effects are not included:

- Earth's oblateness and the associated J2 perturbation.
- Atmospheric drag.
- Solar radiation pressure.
- Third-body gravitational perturbations.
- Precise Earth orientation and precession-nutation effects.

The spherical Earth approximation and simplified Earth rotation are sufficient for an introductory ground-track analysis, but more accurate mission prediction requires additional modelling.

## 11. Conclusion

Ground-track analysis connects orbital mechanics with geographical observation.

By transforming spacecraft positions into Earth-fixed coordinates, it becomes possible to visualise satellite trajectories relative to Earth's surface.

The five spacecraft in Variant 7 have different RAAN values, resulting in five differently oriented orbital planes.
